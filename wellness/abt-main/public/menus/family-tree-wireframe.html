Family Tree App — Wireframes

A grey-box sketch of the screens and how they connect, meant for agreeing on layout and flow before any visual design. Tap a screen name to move between them, or use the left and right arrow keys.

What changed in this version
  • The tree is now drawn properly: couples are joined, children hang from the couple, and generations sit on their own rows. The earlier sketch put aunts and uncles on the same row as you and skipped the parents.
  • The tree scrolls and zooms, has a search box, and a way to jump back to yourself.
  • Empty slots in the tree are tappable, so people can grow the tree from the tree itself instead of going back to a form.
  • Tapping someone shows a preview card with the relationship worked out and the reasoning behind it.
  • Added the first-run screen, which was missing — it's the hardest moment in an app like this.
  • Relationship type is now a set of buttons with a plain-English preview sentence, not a dropdown.

What happens here

  • Each person has their own login, tied to their own place in the tree — that's what makes the app able to say "your 1st cousin" rather than just showing names.
  • An invite code is the only way into an existing family, so a tree can't be joined by strangers.

Open question: when someone joins by invite, are they already in the tree as an unclaimed person, or do they add themselves? Claiming an existing person avoids duplicates.

What happens here

  • Generations sit on their own rows, labelled down the side, so the shape of the family is readable before any name is.
  • Couples are joined by a short bar, and children hang from the bar rather than from one parent. That one rule is what makes cousins and in-laws readable at a glance.
  • Only parent, child and partner links are ever entered. Uncle, cousin, niece and in-law are worked out from those — nobody types "cousin" anywhere.
  • Tapping anyone shows the card below the tree, with the relationship and the reasoning behind it. Tapping a dashed slot starts adding that person, already connected.
  • Zoom and drag replace the old "generations shown" stepper — people expect to move around a tree, not to configure it.

To decide: at around 40 people this layout will outgrow a phone screen. The likely answer is showing three generations around whoever is centred, and re-centring as you tap — worth testing before build.

What happens here

  • Only the first name is required. Half-remembered relatives are the normal case, so the form has to accept them.
  • A partial date is accepted — forcing a full birthdate is the fastest way to lose a great-grandparent.
  • The nickname field matters more than it looks: families search for "Uncle Bob", not "Robert".
  • The button names the next step, so nobody expects saving to be the end.

Needs handling: a duplicate check after the name is typed — two people adding the same grandmother is the most likely way this tree breaks.

What happens here

  • Only three link types exist. Everything else in the family is derived, which keeps the data clean and the form short.
  • The sentence above the button is the whole point of this screen: it shows what the app concluded, in plain words, before anything is saved.
  • Offering the second parent automatically saves a step for the common case and still allows a single parent.
  • Someone can be left unconnected — a floating relative is better than a wrong link.

Still to design: what happens when a link contradicts the dates, such as a parent born after their child. A blocking error is probably too strict.

What happens here

  • The relationship line is calculated, and the sentence underneath explains how. Without the explanation, a surprising result looks like a bug.
  • "Centre tree on Amy" is the same action as in the tree view and keeps the same name in both places.
  • Missing information is stated plainly rather than hidden, since an obvious gap is an invitation to fill it.
  • Showing who added someone gives the family a way to settle disagreements among themselves.
